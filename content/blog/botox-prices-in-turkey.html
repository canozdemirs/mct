What are the types of Botox?

Injections of the "neurotoxins" wrinkle-relaxing botulinum toxin type A are the most popular non-invasive aesthetic procedure. Although they are frequently referred to as "Botox," the FDA has approved four separate neurotoxins: Botox, Dysport, Jeuveau, and Xeomin. The biggest difference between them is in their formulation, which affects how much of the medication is required, how quickly a patient might notice effects, and how much of the product spreads. For instance, a companion protein is included in the formulation of Botox Cosmetic. Similar proteins are present in Dysport, Jeuveau, and Xeomin, however, no companion proteins are present in Xeomin. The FDA has approved all four products, and they have all been used safely and successfully for some time. The appropriate product for you will rely on your individual anatomy, your objectives, and the advice of your cosmetic surgeon.

What is the working mechanism?

Botox injections function by paralyzing or weakening particular muscles or by obstructing particular nerves. An injection of the toxin onobotulinumtoxinA, which is used in Botox, blocks specific chemical nerve signals that cause muscles to contract. By this way, the muscle gets temporarily paralyzed. For instance, the facial muscles that cause wrinkles around the eyes and on the forehead are frequently temporarily relaxed using these injections.

Who are the ideal patients?

Not everyone is a good candidate for Botox. To begin with, there is an age restriction. The ideal age range for Botox is between 18 and 65. If your general health is bad, your skin is unusually thick, or you already have a muscular weakness where the injection is planned, you might not be a good candidate for Botox. More importantly, skin type is a very crucial factor that affects whether you are suitable for the operation or not. In patients with sensitive skin, an allergic reaction at the injection site is likely. For this reason, if you believe your skin is particularly sensitive, you might want to consider obtaining an allergy test before getting Botox. Anyone who may be allergic to the ingredients used in the procedure or who is currently taking aminoglycoside drugs should not undergo Botox treatments. If you get any infections close to the injection site, inform your doctor immediately. Also, if you have an infection in the place where the medicine will be injected, you shouldn't use Botox. As a special case, if you are currently suffering from a bladder infection or are unable to urinate, you should not use this medication to treat hyperactive bladder or incontinence (unless you routinely use a catheter). If you are breastfeeding or pregnant, you shouldn't use Botox.

What should the precautions and expectations be throughout Botox procedure?

Before the operation

Exercise and hot foods and beverages should be avoided for 30 minutes prior to the injection. Deodorant and antiperspirant use should be avoided for 24 hours before and following the injection. If you are getting treatment for excessive perspiration shave your underarms about a day before your injection. Also, in this circumstance, you might want to numb your skin. Your doctor may choose from a number of different techniques to make the area numb, including topical anesthetic, ice, and vibration anesthesia, which employs massage to ease the pain.

During operation

In most cases, Botox injections are carried out in a doctor's office. Tiny doses of botulinum toxin are injected into your skin or muscles by the doctor using a small needle. The size of the area that needs to be treated as one of many variables that affect how many injections are required. The majority of patients experience just minor discomfort during the procedure.

Post-operation

For 24 hours, avoid massaging or rubbing the treated areas. This might aid in limiting the toxin's ability to spread to new areas. After the operation, you can immediately resume your regular activities.

How effective are Botox injections?

An injection of Botox has transient effects. Depending on what you are treating, the effects can last anywhere from three to twelve months. In three months, your symptoms can come back in full force. Especially if your body produces antibodies to the botulinum toxin, it can take less time between injections for your symptoms to come back. Each Botox injection should be spaced out by at least three months to have long-lasting results. Some certain types of drugs may alter the effectiveness of Botox. For this reason, tell your doctor about all other medications you use, particularly: an anti-muscle-tensor; allergy or cold medication; sleeping pills; a medication for injection; warfarin, coumadin, or jantoven, a blood thinner; or Alteplase, clopidogrel, dipyridamole, ticlopidine, and other blood clot prevention medications.
