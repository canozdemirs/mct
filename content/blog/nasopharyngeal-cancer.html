What is nasopharyngeal cancer?

A rare kind of head and neck cancer is nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC), also referred to as nasopharyngeal carcinoma. The tissue that runs from the back of your nose to the back of your mouth is impacted. The nasopharynx is the region at the base of your skull that is situated directly above the roof of your mouth. Before entering your lungs, air passes through your throat, nasopharynx, and nose when you breathe in through your nose. The onset of nasopharyngeal cancer is caused by aberrant cells in the nasopharynx that proliferate uncontrollably and develop into malignant tumors that can spread to your liver, lungs, lymph nodes, and bones.

What are the types of nasopharyngeal cancer?

Your nasopharynx is made up of several tissue types, each of which has a unique cell type. A microscope shows the differences in appearance between each type of cell. Subtypes of Nasopharyngeal Cancer are categorized based on the kind of cell impacted. The World Health Organization (WHO) divides non-communicable cancer (NPC) into three subtypes:
  • Keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (WHO type 1): Keratin-coated cancer cells in the nasopharyngeal lining. Your nails and hair include keratin, a protein.
  • Nonkeratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (WHO type 2): Cancer cells in non-keratinized tissues of the nasopharynx.
  • Poorly or undifferentiated carcinoma, encompassing anaplastic variations and lymphoepithelioma (WHO type 3): Under a microscope, they are cancer cells that have a drastically different appearance from healthy cells. Cancer cells that are poorly differentiated or undifferentiated proliferate and spread swiftly.

What are the risk factors of nasopharyngeal cancer?

One kind of head and neck cancer that starts in the upper throat behind the nose, or nasopharynx, is called nasopharyngeal carcinoma. A higher risk of developing nasopharyngeal cancer has been linked to several risk factors. It is noteworthy that the presence of one or more risk factors does not guarantee the development of cancer in an individual; in fact, a considerable number of individuals with known risk factors never experience the disease.
  • Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) Infection: Nasopharyngeal cancer is significantly linked to Epstein-Barr virus infection, a common virus that causes infectious mononucleosis. Patients with this cancer commonly have EBV DNA found in their blood or nasopharyngeal tissue.
  • Ethnicity and Family History: People of Southern Chinese, Southeast Asian, North African, and Eskimo/Inuit heritage are disproportionately affected by nasopharyngeal cancer. Furthermore, the risk is increased if you have a parent, sibling, or kid who is a first-degree family who has nasopharyngeal cancer.
  • Age and Gender: Adults are more likely to get nasopharyngeal cancer, and the risk rises with age. In general, men are more likely than women to get this malignancy. Consumption of
  • Preserved Foods and Salted Fish: A higher risk of nasopharyngeal cancer has been associated with high consumption of preserved foods and salted fish, especially in Southeast Asia. Nitrosamines, which are recognized carcinogens, might be present in several foods.
  • Use of Tobacco and Alcohol: Although there isn't as much of a correlation between these two lifestyle factors and nasopharyngeal cancer as there is with other head and neck cancers, they may nevertheless raise the risk.
  • Hereditary Reasons: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma may arise due to several hereditary reasons. There may be a genetic component and sensitivity that raise the risk.
People with established risk factors should be particularly cautious about scheduling routine screenings and check-ups, especially if they have a family history of the condition or come from a certain ethnic background. Early detection and intervention can greatly increase the likelihood of a favorable outcome from treatment. It is best to speak with a medical expert if you are worried about your risk of nasopharyngeal cancer. They can offer you individualized advice based on your unique circumstances.

What are the symptoms of nasopharyngeal cancer?

People who have nasopharyngeal cancer typically become aware of a bump on the back of their neck. One or more lumps may be present, and they are typically not uncomfortable. When cancer spreads to your neck's lymph nodes and causes them to swell, these lumps manifest. There are numerous additional cautionary indicators as well, like:
  • Tinnitus, or ringing in the ears
  • Loss of hearing
  • Persistent infections in the ears
  • Headaches
  • Stuffy nose
  • Nosebleeds
  • Having trouble opening your mouth
  • Face numbness
  • Breathing or speech difficulties
Numerous symptoms of nasopharyngeal carcinoma are similar to those of other, less dangerous conditions. It is not always the case that having one or more of these symptoms indicates nasopharyngeal cancer. However, if your symptoms persist or worsen over more than two weeks, you should consult a doctor.

How to diagnose nasopharyngeal cancer?

Your doctor will perform a thorough physical examination and inquire about any family history if they suspect you may have nasopharyngeal cancer. Your nasopharynx, head, neck, mouth, throat, nose, facial muscles, and lymph nodes will all be examined. They might assess hearing. Additional examinations could be,
  • Test for Epstein-Barr virus. You will undergo testing to determine the level of Epstein-Barr virus in your blood because nasopharyngeal cancer is frequently associated with this virus.
  • Biopsy
  • CT scan, or computed tomography
  • A scan using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
  • Scanning with positron emission tomography (PET).
  • Test findings are used by providers to stage nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Systems for staging cancer assist healthcare professionals in prognostication and therapy planning.

What Are the Stages of Nasopharyngeal Cancer and How Much Does Treatment Cost?

Nasopharyngeal cancer is staged according to the size of the tumor, its exact location, and how far it has spread to nearby tissues, lymph nodes, or distant organs. These factors are essential not only for determining the most effective treatment approach but also for estimating the overall cost of treatment.

At Stage 0, cancer cells are present only in the outermost layer of the nasopharynx. The disease has not invaded deeper tissues, making treatment relatively simple. At this stage, close monitoring or localized radiotherapy is usually sufficient, and treatment costs are generally low.

In Stage 1, the tumor has extended beyond the nasopharynx to nearby areas such as the nasal cavity or the back of the throat, but it has not spread to the lymph nodes. Radiotherapy alone is often effective, and because treatment is limited in scope, costs remain relatively affordable.

During Stage 2, cancer spreads to one or more lymph nodes on one side of the neck. This regional spread typically requires a combination of radiotherapy and chemotherapy. As treatment becomes more complex and lasts longer, the overall cost increases compared to earlier stages.

At Stage 3, lymph nodes on both sides of the neck are affected, indicating more advanced regional involvement. Treatment usually involves advanced radiotherapy techniques combined with chemotherapy and requires closer follow-up care. As a result, treatment costs are higher due to the intensity and duration of therapy.

Stage 4 represents the most advanced form of nasopharyngeal cancer. At this stage, the tumor may spread to the lower neck, salivary glands, cranial nerves, eyes, or skull, and in some cases to distant organs such as the lungs or liver. Treatment often includes chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and supportive or palliative care. Because of the complexity and long-term nature of treatment, costs are highest at this stage.

Overall, the cost of nasopharyngeal cancer treatment varies significantly depending on the stage at diagnosis, the type of treatment required, and the duration of care. Early detection not only improves treatment success but also helps keep treatment costs lower, while advanced stages typically require more comprehensive and expensive medical care.

What are the treatment types of nasopharyngeal cancer?

The stage of the cancer, the patient's general health, and personal preferences are some of the variables that affect the course of treatment for nasopharyngeal cancer. To create a thorough treatment plan, a multidisciplinary approach comprising experts like surgeons, radiation oncologists, and oncologists is frequently used. The following are typical methods of treating nasopharyngeal cancer,

Radiation Treatment

  • External Beam Radiation: The most popular type of radiation therapy for nasopharyngeal cancer is external beam radiation. From outside the body, high-energy beams are used to target and destroy malignant cells.
  • Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT): IMRT is a more accurate type of external beam radiation that minimizes radiation exposure to nearby healthy tissues while delivering high radiation doses to the tumor.

Chemotherapy

  • Concurrent Chemoradiation: To increase the efficacy of the treatment, chemotherapy medications may be administered concurrently with radiation therapy.
  • Neoadjuvant or Adjuvant Chemotherapy: To help manage the cancer, chemotherapy may be given either before (neoadjuvant) or after (adjuvant) radiation therapy or surgery.

Surgery

In some circumstances, surgery may be considered, especially if the tumor is localized or if the patient has early-stage nasopharyngeal cancer.
  • Endoscopic Surgery: Tumors from the nasopharynx may be removed with minimally invasive techniques that use an endoscope.

Targeted Therapy

  • Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) Inhibitors: Certain receptors on cancer cells can be targeted by medications like cetuximab to stop their growth signals.

Immunotherapy

  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors: Medication like nivolumab or pembrolizumab stimulates the immune system to attack and kill cancer cells.

Clinical Examinations

  • Clinical trial participation: Certain patients may be qualified to take part in studies examining novel therapies or combinations of treatments.

Supportive Care

  • Palliative care: This type of treatment is intended to relieve symptoms, enhance quality of life, and offer emotional support when a cure is not possible due to an advanced malignancy.
The cancer's stage, location, general health, and other personal considerations all influence the therapy option. Treatment plans are frequently customized to the unique circumstances of each patient, and a mix of therapies may be employed. To make an informed decision about their care, people with nasopharyngeal cancer should talk to their healthcare team about treatment options, possible side effects, and expected outcomes.
