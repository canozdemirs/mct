What is cancer?

Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and division of abnormal cells in the body. Under normal conditions, cells grow, divide, and die in a regulated cycle. However, in cancer, this process becomes disrupted, causing abnormal cells to multiply uncontrollably. Over time, these cells may form a mass known as a tumor, which can be either benign (non-cancerous) or malignant (cancerous).

Malignant tumors are especially serious because they can invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body through the bloodstream or lymphatic system a process called metastasis. This ability to spread makes cancer a potentially life-threatening condition that requires timely and specialized treatment.

Cancer can develop in almost any organ or tissue, and there are more than 100 different types of cancer, each with distinct characteristics and treatment approaches. Common cancer types include breast cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, and leukemia.

What is cervical cancer?

Cervical cancer originates in the cervix, which is the lower part of the uterus (womb) that connects to the upper end of the vagina. It is one of the most common types of cancer in women. Still, it is also highly preventable and treatable if detected early.

What are the types of cervical cancer?

Cervical cancer types depend on the cell type they originate from. The two main types of cervical cancer are squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Additionally, there are other rare subtypes and variants. 

Squamous Cell Carcinoma

This is the most common type of cervical cancer, accounting for approximately 70% to 90% of cases. It develops from the squamous epithelial cells lining the cervix. Squamous cell carcinoma is often linked to infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types.

Adenocarcinoma

Adenocarcinoma of the cervix accounts for about 10% to 30% of cervical cancer cases. It originates in the glandular cells of the cervix, which produce mucus. Adenocarcinoma is also associated with HPV infection. Below you can find the rare subtypes and variants:

Adenosquamous Carcinoma

This is a less common subtype that contains both squamous and glandular cancer cells. 

Small Cell Carcinoma

Small cell carcinoma of the cervix is a rare and aggressive type of cervical cancer. It is characterized by small, round cells and tends to grow and spread quickly.

Neuroendocrine Carcinoma

Another rare and aggressive subtype, neuroendocrine carcinoma, arises from hormone-producing cells in the cervix. This type can be challenging to diagnose and treat. It's important to note that the treatment and management of these types of cervical cancer can vary, and prognosis may also differ based on the specific subtype and stage of the disease. Regular cervical screening and early detection remain crucial for improving outcomes, regardless of the subtype.

What causes cervical cancer?

Cervical cancer is primarily caused by persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV). HPV is a virus and it can be transmitted to another person via sexual contact. Not all HPV infections lead to cervical cancer; however, certain high-risk HPV types can increase the risk of developing cervical cancer by causing changes in the cells of the cervix. These changes can progress to precancerous lesions and, if left untreated, may eventually develop into cervical cancer. Other factors that can contribute to the development of cervical cancer include:

HPV Infection

As mentioned, certain types of HPV are strongly associated with cervical cancer. HPV types 16 and 18 are the most high-risk for causing cervical cancer.

Lack of Regular Screening

Not getting regular Pap tests or cervical screenings can lead to undetected changes in the cervix that could progress to cancer over time.

Smoking

Smoking increases the likelihood of HPV infection and interfere with the body's immune response to the virus.

Weakened Immune System

Women with weakened immune systems, such as those with HIV/AIDS or taking immunosuppressive medications, may be at increased risk.

Long-Term Use of Birth Control Pills

Some studies suggest a possible link between the long-term use of certain types of birth control pills and an increased risk of cervical cancer.

Multiple Sexual Partners

Having multiple sexual partners or engaging in high-risk sexual behaviors can increase the risk of exposure to HPV.

How is cervical cancer diagnosed?

Cervical cancer is typically diagnosed through a combination of screenings, tests, and procedures. To have an effective treatment, early detection is very important, as for any cancer. The diagnostic process may include:

Pap Test (Pap Smear)

During a pap smear, a sample from the cervix is taken and examined under a microscope. This examination will detect abnormal changes. The Pap test can identify precancerous cells or early-stage cervical cancer.

HPV Test

This test checks for high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV) in cervical cells. HPV testing is often done with a Pap test, especially for women over a certain age.

Colposcopy

If the Pap test or HPV test results are abnormal, a colposcopy may be recommended. During this procedure, a special magnifying instrument called a colposcope is used to examine the cervix for any abnormalities closely.

Biopsy

Biopsy may be needed if the results of colposcopy are negative. A small tissue sample is removed from the cervix and sent to a laboratory for further analysis. Biopsy results can confirm whether cervical cancer or precancerous changes are present.

Cervical Cone Biopsy (Conization)

In some cases, a larger piece of tissue from the cervix may be removed in a procedure called conization. This allows for a more detailed examination of the tissue and can help determine the extent of the abnormal changes.

Imaging Tests

If cervical cancer is suspected, imaging tests such as ultrasound, MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), or CT (computed tomography) scans may be used to assess the extent of the cancer and whether it has spread to nearby tissues or lymph nodes.

Staging

When the doctor is assured that patient has cervical cancer, some additional test will be done to determine the stage of the cancer. Staging will give more detailed information about the extent of the disease. It's important to understand that patients medical history, their symptoms and the results of the tests will determine the right treatment and it will be specific for the patient. Regular cervical screenings, such as Pap tests and HPV tests, play a critical role in detecting precancerous changes or early-stage cervical cancer before symptoms develop. Early detection and timely treatment can greatly improve the prognosis and outcome for individuals with cervical cancer.

What are the symptoms of cervical cancer?

There may be no noticeable symptoms in the early stages of cervical cancer, which is why regular cervical screenings are essential for early detection. Symptoms start to be seen as cancer develops. It's important to be aware of these symptoms and seek medical attention if they occur, especially if they are persistent or unusual. The symptoms of cervical cancer may include:
  • Unusual bleeding, such as bleeding between periods, after sexual intercourse, or after menopause, can be a sign of cervical cancer.
  • A change in the amount, color, odor, or consistency of vaginal discharge may be a symptom.
  • Persistent pain or discomfort in the pelvic area or lower abdomen, which may radiate to the back or legs, can occur as the cancer progresses.
  • Some women with cervical cancer may experience pain or discomfort during urination.
  • Pain or discomfort during sexual intercourse, or dyspareunia, can be a symptom.
  • Cervical cancer can cause changes in menstrual patterns, including heavier, longer, or irregular periods.
  • Weight loss that is not attributed to changes in diet or physical activity can sometimes be associated with advanced cervical cancer.
  • In advanced stages, cervical cancer can cause swelling in the legs due to the blockage of blood vessels by the tumor.
  • Feeling fatigued, often accompanied by other symptoms, can be a sign of advanced cancer.
These symptoms are not specific to cervical cancer and can be caused by various other conditions. However, suppose you experience any of these symptoms. In that case, especially if they persist for more than a few weeks, it's recommended to consult a healthcare professional for a proper evaluation and diagnosis. 

How is cervical cancer treated?

The treatment of cervical cancer depends on several factors, including the cancer stage, the type of cervical cancer, the patient's overall health, and their preferences. Cervical cancer treatment often involves a combination of approaches, including surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Treatment aims to eliminate or control the cancer while preserving as much normal tissue and function as possible. Here are the main treatment options:

Surgery

Conization (Cervical Cone Biopsy) A cone-shaped piece of tissue containing the cancerous area may be removed in the early stages. Radical Hysterectomy Surgical removal of the uterus, cervix, and nearby tissues may be recommended for certain early-stage cancers. Pelvic Exenteration In advanced cases, this extensive surgery may involve the removal of the uterus, cervix, vagina, nearby lymph nodes, and sometimes other organs such as the bladder or rectum.

Radiation Therapy

External Beam Radiation High-energy rays are directed at the cancer from outside the body. Brachytherapy Radioactive sources are placed inside or near the cervix to deliver radiation directly to the cancer.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy involves using drugs to destroy cancer cells throughout the body. It can be given before or after surgery or along with radiation therapy.

Targeted Therapy

Targeted therapies specifically target cancer cells by interfering with specific molecules involved in the growth and spread of cancer.

Immunotherapy

With immunotherapy, cancer cells will be recognized and destroyed by the immune cells. It is being studied as a treatment option for cervical cancer. The choice of treatment depends on the cancer's stage, size, and location, as well as the patient's overall health. Some patients may receive a combination of treatments, known as multimodal therapy. Individuals with cervical cancer need to have a thorough discussion with their medical team to determine the most appropriate treatment plan. How to prevent cervical cancer?

Cervical cancer is a highly preventable disease, and several strategies can significantly reduce the risk of developing it. Here are some important steps you can take to prevent cervical cancer:
  • Vaccination against human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most effective ways to prevent cervical cancer. The HPV vaccine protects against the most common high-risk HPV types that cause cervical cancer. The vaccine is most effective when administered before individuals become sexually active.
  • Regular cervical screenings, such as Pap tests (Pap smears) and HPV tests, are crucial for detecting precancerous changes in the cervix before they develop into cancer. Follow your healthcare provider's recommended screening schedule based on age and risk factors.
  • Practicing safe sex by using condoms consistently and correctly can reduce the risk of HPV infection and other sexually transmitted infections.
  • Reducing the number of sexual partners can lower the risk of exposure to HPV and other sexually transmitted infections.
  • Delaying the onset of sexual activity can reduce the risk of HPV infection.
  • Smoking is associated with an increased risk of cervical cancer. Quitting smoking can lower your risk and improve overall health.
  • Maintaining a healthy weight, eating a balanced diet rich in fruits and vegetables, staying physically active, and managing stress can contribute to overall well-being and reduce cancer risk.
  • Regular visits to your healthcare provider for well-woman exams and screenings can help ensure any potential issues are detected and managed early.
  • Certain infections, such as hepatitis B, can increase the risk of cervical cancer. Talk to your healthcare provider about appropriate vaccinations if you are at risk.
