What is Jaw Surgery?

Jaw surgery, also known as orthognathic surgery, is a procedure that corrects a variety of functional and aesthetic problems related to the jaw and facial bones. It typically involves repositioning the upper jaw (maxilla), lower jaw (mandible), or both, to improve the bite, alignment, and overall function of the mouth and teeth. The jaw surgery procedure can be life-changing, improving both the function and appearance of the jaw. Why Choose to Undergo Jaw Surgery ?

  • Correcting Bite Issues:
Underbite: When the lower jaw protrudes beyond the upper jaw. Overbite: When the upper jaw extends too far forward over the lower jaw. Crossbite: When the upper and lower teeth don’t align properly on one side. Open Bite: When the upper and lower teeth don’t meet when the mouth is closed.
  • Facial Aesthetics:
Some people opt for jaw surgery for cosmetic reasons, such as enhancing their facial profile, improving symmetry, or balancing features like the chin and cheekbones.  
  • Breathing and Sleep Apnea:
If a person has difficulty breathing through their nose or suffers from obstructive sleep apnea, jaw surgery may be recommended to help open the airway for better airflow.  
  • TMJ (Temporomandibular Joint) Disorders:
Some jaw issues are linked to jaw joint pain, locking, or difficulty chewing. Surgery might be necessary to treat these conditions.  
  • Correcting Trauma or Injury:
In cases of jaw fractures or other injuries, surgery can help realign the bones for proper healing.

The Procedure of Jaw Surgery

The procedure for jaw surgery, also known as orthognathic surgery, is generally performed in a hospital or specialized surgical center and involves a series of carefully planned steps.
  • Pre-Surgery Preparation:
Before the actual surgery takes place, several key steps occur to ensure that you’re fully prepared:   Consultations and Assessments:
  • Initial Consultation: You’ll meet with an oral and maxillofacial surgeon to discuss your medical history, concerns, and goals for the surgery. The surgeon will conduct a thorough examination of your jaw, teeth, and facial structure.
  • Imaging and X-rays: Detailed imaging like X-rays, CT scans, or 3D imaging will be taken to assess the jaw alignment and identify the exact issues. These images help the surgeon plan the procedure in detail.
  • Orthodontic Consultation: Often, jaw surgery requires orthodontic treatment (braces) before and after the procedure to align the teeth properly. You’ll work with an orthodontist to prepare for this phase.
Braces or Aligners (Pre-Surgery): If you have severe jaw misalignment, your surgeon may recommend wearing braces for 6 to 18 months before surgery to move your teeth into a position that will support the new jaw alignment. This orthodontic phase is crucial to achieving the best results.
  • The Day of Surgery:
On the day of the procedure, you will be admitted to the hospital or surgical center for the surgery.   Anesthesia: General anesthesia is used for jaw surgery, so you’ll be completely asleep and pain-free during the operation. This ensures that the surgeon can work without any discomfort for you. Surgical Procedure:
  • Incisions: The surgeon will make precise incisions, either inside the mouth (no visible scars) or along the gums, to access the jawbones. In some cases, small incisions may also be made on the face.
  • Jaw Repositioning: The surgeon will carefully reposition the upper jaw (maxilla), lower jaw (mandible), or both. Depending on the issue, the surgeon may need to move the jaw forward, backward, or to the side.
Upper jaw surgery may involve cutting the jawbone and shifting it into the correct position. Lower jaw surgery may involve cutting the jawbone and repositioning it to correct misalignment.
  • Securing the Jaws: The repositioned jawbones are then secured with plates, screws, or wires to hold them in place while they heal. These materials are typically permanent, although in rare cases, they may need to be removed later.
  • Soft Tissue Adjustments: The surgeon may also adjust the soft tissues, such as the gums and muscles, to accommodate the new bone position.
Closing the Incisions: Once the jaw has been repositioned and secured, the surgeon will close the incisions with sutures (stitches). If the incisions were made inside the mouth, you will not have visible scars.
  • Post-Surgery Recovery:
Immediately After Surgery:
  • Hospital Stay: Typically, you will stay in the hospital for 1 to 2 days for observation and pain management. You may experience swelling, bruising, and some discomfort during this initial period, which is normal.
  • Pain Management: Pain is usually managed with prescription medication, and ice packs may be applied to reduce swelling.
Post-Operative Care:
  • Dietary Restrictions: For the first few weeks after surgery, you will need to follow a liquid or soft-food diet as your jaw heals. This helps reduce strain on the jaw and encourages proper healing.
  • Oral Hygiene: Oral care becomes a bit more challenging post-surgery, so you’ll need to be extra cautious about keeping your mouth clean. Your surgeon or orthodontist will give you guidelines on how to clean your mouth without disturbing the surgical site.
  • Swelling and Bruising: Swelling and bruising around the face and neck are common and can last for several weeks. Applying ice packs during the first few days can help reduce swelling.
Follow-Up Appointments: You will need to attend regular follow-up appointments to ensure proper healing and monitor your progress. These visits typically happen within the first few days after surgery and then again after a few weeks and months. The orthodontist will also monitor your progress and may adjust your braces or aligners if needed.
  • Long-Term Recovery:
  • Gradual Improvement: While the initial recovery period is usually 6 to 12 weeks, full recovery can take 6 to 12 months. During this time, your jaw will continue to heal, and your orthodontist will work on adjusting your braces to maintain proper alignment.
  • Physical Therapy: Some patients may need physical therapy to help regain full jaw mobility and function, especially if there was a significant repositioning.
  • Jaw Function and Appearance: Over time, you will notice significant improvements in both the function of your jaw (such as ease of chewing, speaking, and breathing) and the appearance of your face, particularly if the surgery was also done for cosmetic reasons.

Risks and Complications

While jaw surgery is generally safe, there are potential risks and complications, including:   Infection: As with any surgery, there is a risk of infection at the incision sites. Nerve Damage: In some cases, patients may experience temporary or permanent numbness or tingling around the mouth, chin, or teeth due to nerve involvement during surgery. Jaw Misalignment: Though rare, there’s a chance that the jaw could shift out of place during recovery, requiring further adjustments. Difficulty with Eating or Speaking: These issues are common in the immediate post-surgery period, but most people regain full functionality as they heal.

Aftercare and Lifestyle Adjustments

Be prepared for a few lifestyle adjustments in the weeks and months following your surgery. These could include changes in your diet, physical activity, and care routine, but with proper follow-up and patience, most people achieve excellent results.
