What is liver cancer?

Cancer is a condition in which the body's cells proliferate uncontrolled. Liver cancer is the term for cancer that first appears in the liver.

How does liver cancer happen?

Although the precise origin of liver cancer is unknown, cirrhosis, or liver damage and scarring, is a common factor in the majority of instances. Numerous factors, including years of heavy alcohol consumption and a protracted hepatitis B or hepatitis C virus infection, can contribute to cirrhosis. Additionally, because these factors can result in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, it is thought that obesity and a poor diet can raise the risk of liver cancer.

What are the symptoms of liver cancer?

Here are some symptoms caused by liver cancer. Please see a doctor if you are experiencing one of them.
  • A hard lump on the right side just below the rib cage
  • Discomfort in the upper abdomen on the right side
  • A swollen abdomen
  • Easy bruising or bleeding
  • Loss of appetite
  • Weight loss for no known reason
  • Fever
  • Unusual tiredness
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Yellowing of the skin.

What are the types of liver cancer?

The main types of liver cancer are hepatocellular carcinoma and bile duct cancer, also known as cholangiocarcinoma. In most of the adult cases, liver cancers are hepatocellular carcinomas. Hepatocellular carcinomas are the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Liver cancer can be seen both in adults and in children. However, treatments differ.

How does diagnosis happen?

Some liver cancers can be detected through screening in high-risk individuals who are asymptomatic, but the majority of liver cancers are discovered when they are already producing symptoms. In order to better understand your symptoms and any risk factors, your doctor will inquire about your medical history. Your doctor will also perform a physical examination on you to check for indications of liver cancer and other health issues. More tests will likely be performed if your symptoms and the physical examination outcomes indicate you may have liver cancer. These could include lab testing, liver tissue biopsies, or imaging examinations. Imaging exams produce images of the interior of your body using X-rays, magnetic fields, or sound waves. Imaging exams may include:

Ultrasound

The first test done to examine the liver is frequently an ultrasound. It produces an image on a computer screen using sound waves. This examination can reveal liver tumors, which can subsequently, if necessary, be examined for malignancy.

CT

An X-ray examination called a CT scan creates precise images of your body. Numerous types of liver tumors can be found with the aid of an abdominal CT scan. Suppose there are any tumors in the liver or elsewhere in the abdomen. In that case, it can provide precise details on their size, shape, and location, as well as any surrounding blood arteries. Additionally, CT scans can accurately direct a biopsy needle into a potential tumor. A chest CT may be performed if it is determined that you have liver cancer in order to check for possible lung cancer spread.

MRI

MRI scans offer precise pictures of the body's soft tissues, just like CT scans. However, MRI scans substitute radio waves and powerful magnets for X-rays. MRI scans are an excellent tool for examining liver cancers. They can sometimes distinguish between benign and malignant tumors. They can also be used to check for blockages in the blood arteries in and around the liver and determine whether liver cancer has migrated to other body organs.

Angiography

An x-ray examination called an angiography examines blood arteries. While X-ray images are being taken, contrast media, or dye, is injected into an artery to define blood vessels. A liver cancer's blood supply can be seen during angiography, which can assist medical professionals in determining whether to remove the malignancy and organize the procedure.

Bone scan

A bone scan can aid in the search for cancer that has metastasized to bones.

What is the treatment?

Primary liver cancer treatments are determined by the disease's severity, age, and general health. Treatment options include:

Surgery

1) Tumor removal with surgery. Suppose your tumor is small and your liver function is good. In that case, your doctor may, in certain cases, advise a procedure to remove the liver cancer and a small part of the healthy liver tissue that surrounds it. Whether this is a choice for you also depends on where in the liver the cancer is located, how effectively your liver works, and how healthy you are in general. 2) Surgery for a liver transplant Your sick liver is removed during a liver transplant procedure and then replaced with a healthy liver from a donor. Only a limited number of persons with early-stage liver cancer have the option of undergoing liver transplant surgery.

Localized treatments

A liver cancer treatment that is administered locally targets the cancer cells themselves or their immediate surroundings. Options for localized liver cancer treatment include warming up cancerous cells and cancer cell freezing,

Radiation therapy

This therapy shrinks tumors by destroying cancer cells with a powerful energy from sources like protons and X-rays. The energy is carefully directed toward the liver while the surrounding healthy tissue is spared. Radiation therapy may be an option if other treatments aren't feasible or haven't worked. Radiation therapy may help manage symptoms of advanced liver cancer.

Targeted drug therapy

Targeted medication therapies concentrate on particular defects that are prevalent in cancer cells. Targeted medication therapies can kill cancer cells by preventing these aberrations. There are numerous specific medications available to treat advanced liver cancer.

Immunotherapy

Your immune system is used in immunotherapy to combat cancer. Because cancer cells create proteins that make immune system cells blind, your body's immune system may fail to combat your cancer. Immunotherapy affects that process in order to work. Advanced liver cancer patients are typically the only ones who receive immunotherapy treatments.

Chemotherapy

Drugs are used in chemotherapy to kill rapidly proliferating cells, especially cancer cells. Chemotherapy can be given orally, intravenously, or both through a vein in your arm. Advanced liver cancer is occasionally treated with chemotherapy. How to reduce the risk of having liver cancer?

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) chronic infection are the major risk factors for liver cancer globally. Some liver cancers could be prevented by not sharing needles and by engaging in safer sex practices, as these viruses can transmit from person to person through unprotected intercourse, childbirth, and sharing contaminated needles. In addition, Alcohol consumption can cause cirrhosis, which can result in liver cancer. Avoiding alcohol altogether or consuming it in moderation may help prevent liver cancer. Some of these malignancies can be avoided by not smoking because it raises the risk of liver cancer. If you smoke, stopping will reduce your risk of developing this cancer and numerous other cancers and serious illnesses.

How does liver cancer surgery happen?

There are two types of liver cancer surgery.

Laparoscopic surgery

Laparoscopic surgery can be used to execute several liver resection procedures through a series of tiny abdominal incisions. In order to observe the liver, a laparoscope—a lit tube with a small camera on it—is inserted into one of the incisions. Subsequently, the malignant area of the liver is removed using additional tools. Laparoscopy frequently results in fewer scars, less discomfort, and a quicker recovery.

Open surgery

Open surgery may be advised for big tumors or those buried deep within the liver. During an open operation, a few-inch-long incision is made across the right side of the abdomen, just below the ribs. Any form of liver surgery may need a five- to seven-day hospital stay. Doctors and nurses treat any discomfort and keep an eye out for infection symptoms. It could take up to six weeks or longer to fully recuperate following surgery.
